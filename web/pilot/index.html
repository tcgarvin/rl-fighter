How the pilot works

A small network flies every ship

Every ship on the live page is flown by the same neural network: fleet pilot v1 · warm, update 400. It is a plain multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers and no attention or memory. Thirty times a second, each ship runs it once on its own view of the battle and gets back a setting for every thruster, turret and trigger it has.

One ship's observation goes in at the top. Two fully connected layers of 256 units, each followed by tanh, turn it into 61 numbers. Those are split into 21 small choices, one per actuator, and the chosen row of actions reaches the ship after a short reaction delay.

What the pilot sees

The observation is a fixed list of 385 numbers built by the Rust sim (crates/sim/src/obs.rs, SIM_SPEC.md §7.2). It is egocentric: every position, velocity and direction is rotated into the ship's own body frame, so "forward" is always the first axis and "port" the second. Each value is divided by a fixed scale (arena radius, 100 m/s, 20 m and so on) and clipped to ±5. A slot with nothing in it is all zeros, with its present flag at 0.

  • Self (21): present, control lock, speed, forward and sideways speed, spin, hull and shield fractions, whether it has a shield, time left, the current power split, mass, speed cap, size, maximum hull, the direction to the arena centre and the distance to its edge, and the reaction delay.
  • Thrusters (8 slots × 7): where each thruster sits, which way it pushes, how hard (as an acceleration), and its current throttle level.
  • Turrets (6 slots × 10, plus an arc and a range per slot at the end): where each gun sits, which way it points now, how far through its reload it is, and its weapon kind (slug, beam, flak, torpedo).
  • Other ships (7 slots × 12), nearest first: friend or foe, relative position and velocity, relative heading, hull and shield fractions, size and distance. A match holds at most 20 ships (a carrier's docked wing counts), so the pilot sees the nearest 7 of up to 19 others. A strike-craft fleet has at most 8 ships (4 a side), where the seven slots cover every other ship; in a carrier battle the farthest drop out of view.
  • Projectiles (8 slots × 8): enemy shots only, sorted by threat rather than distance: relative position and velocity and damage. The sim works out when each shot will pass closest and how close it will come, and lists those two numbers in the record too.
  • Pending actions (4 slots × 22): the actions already chosen but not yet applied (see the reaction delay below). With them and the throttle levels in view, the pilot needs no memory to know what its ship is about to do.

Nothing in the observation says "you are a gunship". The pilot learns which hull it is flying from the thruster and turret records: their count, placement and strength, and whether the guns turn. That is what lets one network fly three different ships, and in principle a fourth.

What it controls

There is no autopilot. The pilot never says "turn left" or "attack that ship"; it sets actuators directly, and turning means firing a pair of side thrusters and then the opposite pair to stop (crates/sim/src/actions.rs, SIM_SPEC.md §7.1). Its output is a row of 21 small integers, one per action head:

headcountchoicesmeaning
throttle 8 3 per thruster slot: lower, hold or raise its level, a sixth per tick (zero to full in 0.2 s)
slew 6 3 per turret slot: turn left, hold or turn right
fire 6 2 per turret slot: pull the trigger or not
power 1 7 a target split of power between shields, engines and weapons, from a fixed menu; the ship shifts toward it gradually

Each head is its own categorical distribution over its slice of the 61 logits (8×3 + 6×3 + 6×2 + 7). The heads are sampled independently, on the live page as in training, so the pilot's moves are a little random by design.

Masks: one set of heads, three hulls

The network always produces all 21 heads. A head whose actuator the hull does not have is masked: its output is ignored and replaced by "hold" or "don't fire", and in training it adds nothing to the loss. Throttle heads are masked for empty thruster slots, slew heads for empty slots and fixed guns, fire heads for empty turret slots. The power head is never masked.

The reaction delay

An action takes effect 2 ticks (67 ms) after the pilot chooses it. It waits in a short queue that the pilot can see in its observation (the pending actions block). The delay makes it fly like a pilot rather than a servo: without it, twitching the nose back and forth every tick to aim is the best strategy, and it looks robotic.

That is the whole pilot: 180,285 numbers, about 721 kB as 32-bit floats. There is no normalisation layer, no convolution, no attention and no recurrent state. Each tick's decision depends only on that tick's observation. The weights start orthogonal, with the output layer scaled down so a fresh network picks every choice about equally often.

In the browser the same arithmetic runs as a plain JavaScript loop over the exported weights (web/src/live/policy.js; a pytest checks it against PyTorch). With 8 ships at 30 ticks a second, that is about 43 million multiply-adds a second, which a phone handles easily.

The critic, used only in training

PPO also trains a critic: a second network that estimates how well things are going for a ship, so that training can tell a good action from a lucky one. It never flies and is not shipped to the browser. Fleet pilot v1 uses a fleet critic (FleetCritic) with 243,394 parameters in three parts:

  • A base value from the ship's own observation, through its own 385 → 256 → 256 → 1 tanh network (164,865). It has the actor's shape but separate weights; nothing is shared between the two.
  • A per-ship encoder, 385 → 64 → 64 with tanh (28,864), run on every ship in the match. The encodings of the ship's live teammates and of the live enemies are averaged separately into two summaries.
  • A correction: the base network's last hidden layer, both summaries and the number of teammates and enemies (386 numbers) go through a 128-unit layer to one number that is added to the base value (49,665). It starts at exactly zero, so the critic first agrees with the one it was warm-started from.

Averaging over a team means the same critic handles one ship a side or four. The critic sees the whole match, while the actor sees only its own ship's view. This split (decentralised actors, a centralised critic) is the usual MAPPO recipe.

How it was trained

Learning to fly from scratch by trial and error failed. In the learnability spike, PPO from random weights never learned to fire. What works is to copy a teacher first, then improve on it. Every hull has a hand-written scripted pilot in Rust that flies the same way, through actuators only. The scripts are the teachers. They never fly on the live page.

  1. Copy (behaviour cloning). Train the network to predict the script's action on states the script visits, with a little noise injected so it also sees how the script recovers. On its own this fails: the spike's cloned fighter won 0.6 % of duels against the script, because small mistakes take it into states the script never showed it.
  2. Correct (DAgger). Let the network fly, ask the script what it would have done in each state the network reached, add those rows to the data and retrain. Three rounds of two million rows each. For fleet pilot v1, that meant fleets of every hull, each labelled by its own script.
  3. Improve (PPO). Reinforcement learning on the match reward (damage dealt and taken, a small cost per shot, a penalty outside the arena, ±1 for winning or losing), with a pull toward the script that fades to zero over the first 300 updates. Hits are credited to the tick the trigger was pulled, not the tick the shot landed.

The champion comes from run 2026-10-30-fleet-pilot (plan). It was warm-started: before DAgger began, the network was given the weights of the previous champion, a fighter-only duel pilot. A second arm started from fresh weights; it did worse and never learned to duel. Then came PPO, with 256 matches at once and 128 ticks per update, over the fleet_v1 mix of rosters: 1 to 4 ships a side, mixed hulls, sometimes uneven. Opponents were the scripts at first, then a mix of scripts, the pilot itself and frozen earlier copies. The champion is update 400 of 600.

Against the scripts, measured after training:

  • it wins 0.89 of fleet matches, and at least 0.74 in every roster class;
  • every hull deals at least 90 % of its script's damage (fighters and strafers far more) and survives longer than its script;
  • it wins 512 of 512 fighter duels against the script, and 0.45 against the previous champion.

The verdict was PARTIAL, and one gate was waived. Before training, the plan required every hull's engine duty (its strongest thruster's average throttle) to be at least 0.15. The champion's fighters fly at 0.13; the script's fly at 0.39. PPO taught them to coast: mostly sit, turn and shoot. That beat both the scripts and the previous champion. The user waived the engine-duty bound after the gates ran, on the view that if flying slowly is the strongest strategy, the pilot should ship and the sim or the reward should be the thing that changes. So expect slow, fairly static fighters on the live page. The full numbers are in the run's results.

What it isn't (yet)

  • No attention. Ships and projectiles sit in fixed slots sorted by distance or threat, and the network sees them as one flat list. It cannot see more than seven other ships or eight shots, and it learns about "the nearest ship" rather than about ships in general.
  • No memory. There is no recurrent state. Anything the pilot knows about the past has to be in the current observation: velocities, reload timers, throttle levels and its own queued actions.
  • No orders. Nothing in the observation says what to do or who to go after. Every pilot fights for the team reward on its own judgement.

What's next

Orders (M3, stage B). docs/M3_PLAN.md adds carriers and a scripted commander that gives each squad an order: attack a target, guard a ship, hold a point. Stage B adds an order block to the pilot's observation: the kind of order, and the target's relative position and velocity. That takes the observation from 385 to about 397 numbers. The new inputs start with zero weights, so before its first update the new pilot is exactly fleet pilot v1, and it learns to follow orders from there. Until then (stage A), v1 will see a carrier as just a big enemy ship.

Attention (M4, after the carriers). docs/DESIGN.md §7 describes the pilot the project is aiming for. Every ship, shot, objective and order becomes a token, and an attention encoder reads over them, with no cap on the number of ships. Each actuator is a query token that outputs its own action, so a hull added later would be flown without new heads. The observation is already built from per-entity records for this reason; the token version is a second assembler over the same record writers.
