How the commander works

A second network gives the orders

On the live page since 2026-11-08, about level with the script

Above the pilots sits a commander: a network that runs a whole team and decides, once a second, which ships fly together, what each squad should do, where the carrier goes and when to launch from it. It never touches a thruster. Its orders reach the pilots through their observations, and the pilots do the flying. Unlike the pilot, it reads the battle with attention, so it can point at one particular ship and say "attack that one" or "guard this one". On the live page each side draws its commander from four: the sim's script and three learned ones (their first night).

In one picture

Once a second the sim hands the commander a table: one row of 40 numbers for each of the 20 ship slots, and 53 numbers about the match. A shared encoder turns each ship's row into 64 numbers. One layer of self-attention lets every ship's vector take in the others. The average over the ships, joined with the match numbers, becomes a summary of 128. Three groups of heads read the summary and the ship vectors, and the 40 integers they choose go back to the sim, which turns them into commands and runs the next 30 ticks.

What the commander sees

The inputs are built by the Rust sim; their layout is agreed in docs/COMMANDER.md. The pilot sees the battle from its own cockpit. The commander sees it from its team's side of the map: the team frame is the world as it is for team 0 and turned half a circle for team 1, so every commander's home is on the left (−x) and the enemy's on the right. Positions are divided by the arena radius, velocities by 100 m/s.

A ship gets a row when it is alive and on the field or jumping in. Ships still docked in a carrier's hangar have no row; the match numbers count them. An empty slot is all zeros, with its valid flag at 0. Rows sit by ship slot, not sorted by distance, so choosing a row means choosing a ship.

  • What it is (8): valid; own team or enemy; its hull class as one of six (fighter, strafer, gunship, carrier, freighter and a spare for a hull added later).
  • Where it is going (6): position, velocity and heading.
  • Its state (5): hull and shield left, as fractions; whether it is on the field, jumping in, or (a transport) charging its jump out.
  • Its squad and order (10, own strike craft only): which squad it is in (none, or 1 to 4), what kind of order that squad is following (none, attack, defend, go to), and how long until it may be moved to another squad.
  • Its part in the plan (11): for each squad, whether this ship is the squad's attack target, and whether it is the squad's defend ward; whether it is an enemy threatening one of my capital ships; its distance to my carrier (or lead transport) and to the enemy's.
  • The mission (6): which role this team plays (deathmatch, carrier battle, convoy defender, convoy attacker), and the time left and gone, as shares of the time limit.
  • My carrier and hangar (8): how many fighters, strafers and gunships are still docked, how many launch bays are ready and how many the carrier has, whether my carrier and the enemy's are still alive, and how long since my last launch.
  • The convoy (5): transports escaped, destroyed, needed to escape, needed to destroy, and charging their jump, each as a share of the convoy. Zeros outside a convoy.
  • My squads (4 × 8): each squad's size, its order kind, how long its order stays locked, and its go-to point if it has one.
  • The field (2): how many strike craft each side has out.

The commander does not see the fight in detail: no shots, no guns, no thrusters. It sees where the ships are, how hurt they are, and what its squads are doing. Nor is it told which pilot flies which ship.

What it decides

Each second the commander makes one decision: a row of 40 small integers, one per head. Nearly every head has a keep choice, which changes nothing, and most of the time keep is the right answer. Whatever is new becomes a command that the sim carries out on the team's next slow tick (crates/sim/src/command.rs).

headcountchoicesmeaning
launch 3 2 per strike hull (fighter, strafer, gunship): hold, or launch one from the carrier's hangar
waypoint 1 26 keep, or steer the carrier to one of the 25 map points
squad 20 6 per ship slot: keep, no squad, or squad 1 to 4
order 4 5 per squad: keep, none, attack, defend, go to
target 4 20 per squad: the ship to attack, by slot
ward 4 20 per squad: the own ship to defend, by slot
point 4 25 per squad: the map point to go to

A target, ward or point head only counts when its squad's order picks that kind. A squad told to defend uses its ward head and ignores the other two, in training as in play. Squads are formed first, then each squad's order applies, so one decision can gather four fighters into a squad and send them after a carrier.

The map points

A go-to order or a carrier waypoint names one of 25 fixed points, not any spot on the map. Point 0 is the centre. The rest sit on 3 rings, at 0.3, 0.6, 0.85 of the arena radius, 8 to a ring and 45 degrees apart, counted counter-clockwise from straight at the enemy. A squad sent to a point is asked to get within 60 m of it.

Masks: only choices that make sense

Before each decision the sim works out which choices are allowed, and a choice that is not gets a score so low it is never picked. Keep and hold are always allowed.

  • Launch a hull only when my carrier is alive, has one of that hull docked, and has a bay ready. Launches go fighter, strafer, gunship, and the sim picks the bay.
  • Waypoint: a map point only while my carrier is alive.
  • Squad: only for my own strike craft on the field or jumping in, and not while that ship's move lock lasts.
  • Order: only while my team has strike craft out and the squad's order lock has run out. Attack also needs an enemy on the field, defend an own ship on the field.
  • Target: enemy ships on the field. Ward: own ships on the field, strike craft or capital ship. Point: every point, always.

Locks: no dithering

A network that decides every second could change its mind every second, and pilots chasing a new order each second get nowhere. So when a squad's order changes (a new kind, target, ward or point), that squad's order head is locked to keep for the next 3 decisions. When a ship moves to another squad, its squad head is locked for 3 too. Repeating the same order is not a change, and launches are never locked. The time left on each lock is in the inputs, so the commander can see when it may act again.

That is the whole commander: 84,676 numbers, about 339 kB as 32-bit floats, smaller than a single pilot. The trunk holds 38,528 of them and the heads 46,148. The summary's 117 inputs are the 64-number average of the ships and the 53 match numbers; a squad-of-ship head reads that ship's vector and the summary (192); a squad query reads the squad's name, its 8 match numbers and the summary (168).

Most of it is shared. The encoder and the squad-of-ship head run with the same weights on every ship, and the squad query with the same weights for every squad, told apart only by a learned 32-number name per squad. So twenty squad heads cost the weights of one.

Attention, plainly

After the encoder, each ship is a list of 64 numbers describing that ship on its own. Attention lets each ship's description take in the rest of the battle. Every ship makes three things from its vector: a question (the query), a label (the key) and a message (the value). Each ship compares its question with every ship's label, and the better a label fits, the more of that ship's message it takes in. It adds the blend to its own vector rather than replacing it (the residual), so it keeps what it knew. Four heads do this side by side, each with questions of 16 numbers, so one head can learn to look at nearby enemies while another looks at the carrier. What each one looks for is up to training.

Ships that are not in play (destroyed, docked, empty slots) are left out of every blend and out of the average. Nothing depends on the order of the rows, so the same network can command a two-ship duel or a twenty-ship carrier battle. The attention is written out by hand rather than with a library layer, so the browser can copy it line by line.

Pointer heads

To attack a ship the commander has to name it, and the ships change every match. A pointer head names one without a fixed menu. For each squad, a query is built from the squad's name, its record and the summary. The attack head turns the query and every ship's vector into 64 numbers each, with its own weights, and multiplies them together number by number and adds up (a dot product, divided by 8): one score per ship. After the mask (enemy ships on the field only), the best-scoring ships are the likeliest picks. The defend head works the same way with weights of its own, over own ships. Either way, "which ship" is answered by matching what the squad needs against what each ship is, and the ship in slot 3 is found exactly as the ship in slot 17 would be.

The critic, used only in training

PPO also trains a critic: a second network that estimates, at each decision, how the match is going for the commander's team, so training can tell a good decision from a lucky one. It has the actor's trunk, with weights of its own, and one number at the end (39,361 parameters). It sees one thing the actor does not: which pilot flies each ship. Every ship's row gets 11 more numbers, one for each of the 10 pilots on the live list and one for "a script" (capital ships), so its rows are 51 wide.

A squad of strong pilots is worth more than the same ships with weaker ones, and the critic can know that, which makes its estimates sharper. The actor has to command whoever it is given, so it gets no such label. The critic never commands anything and will not be shipped to the browser.

How it is trained

By reinforcement learning with PPO (spacefight/commander/train.py), where one step is one decision: one second of a match. There is no copying stage. The commander starts from random weights, under pilots that already know how to fight.

  1. The match. Each match draws a mission (deathmatch, carrier battle or convoy, equally often) and a roster as on the live page, and puts the learner on a random side. Every strike craft on both sides gets a pilot drawn at random, ship by ship, from the 10 on the live list. The pilots are frozen: they do not learn while the commander does. Capital ships fly the sim's scripts.
  2. The enemy commander. The scripted commander 35 % of the time, the random one 10 %, the learner itself 30 %, and one of its own earlier snapshots 25 %. A snapshot is saved every 20 updates and the latest 10 are kept. The ones it beats least are drawn most: each is weighted by one minus the learner's score against it, plus 0.1 so none is forgotten. Until the first snapshot, their share goes to the learner itself.
  3. The reward. +1 for a win, −1 for a loss and 0 for a draw at the end, by each mission's own rules. Between those, a small running score: the team's lead in value (each side's hull and shield points left, summed) as a share of both teams' value at the start, times 0.5. Each decision is paid the change in that lead. Because it is paid as a change in a score (a potential-based shaping), the payments cancel over a match: they cannot change which plan is best, only how soon the commander hears that a decision went well.
  4. The update. 4,096 decisions per update, 4 passes over them in batches of 512, clip 0.2, a discount of 0.99 per decision (a horizon of about 100 seconds) and GAE λ 0.95. Against itself, both sides' decisions are training data.

Commanders and pilots are meant to take turns: commanders train over frozen pilots, then pilots train under frozen commanders (docs/PILOT_TRAINING.md). This is the first commander phase. The scripted commander stays in the stable of opponents until learned ones beat it.

What it isn't (yet)

  • Better than the script. After one night the learned commanders are about level with it, and so is one that gives random orders: most of today's pilots ignore orders, so orders barely change a match. They run on the live page beside the script all the same (?commands=1 draws their orders), and the next pilots are trained to follow.
  • In charge of the transports. Convoy transports stay on their scripted route; their waypoint is never the commander's. Of the capital ships, it steers only its carrier.
  • Aware of who flies a ship. Its inputs say what each ship is, not which pilot is in it. Only the critic knows, and only in training.
  • A memory. It decides from what it sees now. The lock timers and the time since its last launch are its only sense of what it did before.
  • Free to go anywhere. It sends squads and the carrier to one of 25 points, a coarse map that is easy to learn and easy to draw.
